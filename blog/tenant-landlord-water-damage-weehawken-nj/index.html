Water Damage in a Rental: Tenant and Landlord Steps

.

Both sides have jobs to do when water gets into a rental. Clear notice and good records make the rest easier.

Call (844) 950-6900Dispatch line open 24/7
.

By the Weehawken Water Damage Pros team · Published 2026

Start with notice in writing

Tenants should tell the landlord or property manager about water damage as soon as it is safe, and follow up in writing. An email or text with photos creates a record. Phone calls are fine in an emergency, but write down the time and what was said.

Landlords should respond promptly, stop the source if it is under their control and arrange drying. This guide is general information, not legal advice. Your lease, local rules and state tenant law decide the details, so ask a qualified professional if you are unsure.

What a tenant can do right away

  1. Protect yourself

    Stay away from wet electrical areas and ceilings that sag. If water is near outlets, call the landlord and an electrician.

  2. Stop the water if you can

    Close a fixture valve under a sink or toilet. Do not force a main valve in a building you do not manage.

  3. Photograph the damage

    Take photos and video of the source and your belongings before moving anything.

  4. Notify the landlord

    Send a written message with photos and ask when they can respond.

  5. Move belongings

    Lift items to dry areas and keep receipts for emergency costs.

  6. Check your renters policy

    Call your insurer to report damage to your belongings or extra living costs.

What a landlord or manager should do

Typical landlord steps

  • Stop the source or shut off the riser.
  • Call a restoration company for extraction and drying.
  • Notify neighboring units that may be affected.
  • Contact the building insurer.
  • Document the cause, the units and the dates.
  • Keep the tenant informed about timing and access.

Fast drying limits what has to be replaced. A landlord who waits risks larger damage, more units affected and disputes. A property manager with a restoration contact on file can start within hours.

Who pays for what

Responsibility often depends on the cause. A failed pipe inside a wall is usually a building issue. A tenant's overflowing tub or appliance may be treated differently. The landlord's policy generally covers the building, while the tenant's renters policy covers their belongings and liability.

Because these questions are fact specific, keep records and let the insurers sort out the details. Avoid signing anything that waives your rights until you understand it. Our page on commercial water damage restoration also covers multi-unit buildings.

Access for drying

Crews need access to the wet unit and sometimes to the units above and below. Landlords should give proper notice, and tenants should help arrange times. Equipment will run for days, so ask about noise, electricity use and how to stay safe around hoses and cords.

If a unit is unlivable, ask in writing about temporary housing, rent and storage. Your lease and your renters policy may address these points.

Mold concerns in rentals

Tenants who see staining or smell dampness should report it quickly. Landlords should look for the moisture source, dry the area and decide whether materials must be removed. Mold remediation explains how containment and removal are handled when growth is widespread.

Records both sides should keep

Keep emails and texts about the leak, dated photos, invoices for emergency work and notes from calls. Tenants should keep receipts for hotels, meals and storage. Landlords should keep contractor invoices, drying logs and insurance claim numbers.

If a dispute arises later, a clear timeline is the strongest tool. It shows when the problem was reported, when action was taken and what it cost.

Common sources in apartments

Leaks in apartments often come from a neighbor's overflowing tub, a failed supply line, a leaking radiator valve, a roof leak or a sprinkler line. Each has a different responsible party. Identify the source first, then notify the right people.

If a heating line or sprinkler is involved, the building engineer or the fire safety contact must be notified immediately. Do not try to handle those yourself.

Preventing the next leak in a rental

Tenants can help by reporting drips early, keeping drains clear and not running appliances unattended. Landlords can help by inspecting supply lines, replacing aging valves and checking roofs and gutters on a schedule.

A short written maintenance routine, shared by both sides, prevents most surprises. It also builds trust, so when something does go wrong, everyone knows who to call and what to do first.

Communicating respectfully under stress

Water damage is disruptive, and tempers can run short. Keep messages short and factual. State what happened, what you need and by when. Thank people who respond quickly. A cooperative tone usually gets faster results than an angry one, and it leaves a better record.

If you cannot reach the landlord, document your attempts. Note the times you called and the messages you sent. That record shows you tried, and it may matter later.

Whatever your role, remember that speed limits the damage. Water that is extracted and dried quickly means fewer repairs, fewer disputes and less time out of your home. A short call to start drying is almost always worth making, even before every question about who pays has an answer. Landlords who act within hours usually keep a small loss small, and tenants who report within hours usually keep their belongings safe and their records clear.

Read your lease first. Many standard leases spell out who handles repairs and how notice must be given, and those terms shape the steps above.

Weehawken rental notes

Many Weehawken renters live in multi-unit buildings, so a leak in one apartment can affect the one below. Report the problem to building management and photograph both units if you can.

Tenants in Jersey City and Fairview are in the same position. Ask your landlord or building manager who their restoration contact is, and keep their number handy.

Questions people ask

Who calls a restoration company, tenant or landlord?

Usually the landlord or manager for building damage. Tenants should report right away and may call for emergencies if they cannot reach anyone.

Does renters insurance cover water damage?

It commonly covers your belongings and some living costs after a covered event. Check your policy and ask your insurer.

Should I move out during drying?

Not always. Ask the crew and landlord what is safe. If the unit is unlivable, ask about housing in writing.

What if the leak came from the unit above?

Report it to building management in writing, with photos. Who pays depends on the cause, the lease and insurance.

Can I withhold rent?

Rules vary and the stakes are high. Ask a qualified professional or local tenant resource before doing so.
